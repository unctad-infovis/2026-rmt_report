    Under current policies, global oil demand is projected to stay far above levels consistent with net-zero emissions. Seaborne trade in low-carbon fuels remains limited.

    Importers can change suppliers as well as routes. As tariff conditions changed, US buyers turned to suppliers in Viet Nam and elsewhere in Asia. The Viet Nam–US route accounted for 1.8% of global container trade in 2025, up from 0.5% in 2015.

For a tanker worth $100 million, the war risk premium reportedly rose within days from about $200,000 to $1 million per voyage.     Red Sea security risks have kept many carriers sailing around the Cape of Good Hope, tying up container ships on longer voyages.     The International Maritime Organization postponed talks on whether to adopt global net-zero rules for shipping until late 2026. Without them, shipowners face different emissions rules depending on where they operate.

    The fleet in service still relies mainly on conventional fuels. But nearly half of the tonnage on order is for ships that can run on alternative fuels – though that share dipped this year, partly because global rules remain unclear.

    About 42% of the world’s ships are over 20 years old. Fleet renewal will create work beyond shipbuilding. Developing countries could take on more of that work by repairing, upgrading and safely recycling older ships.

    Higher shipping costs don’t stay with shipping companies. Passed on to importers through freight rates and surcharges, they can raise the price of imported food, energy and fertilizer. If they last, UNCTAD estimates they would push up consumer prices and reduce purchasing power, especially in small island developing states and the least developed countries.

From February to August 2026, container rates from Shanghai to the Persian Gulf and Red Sea rose from about $1,075 to a record $5,637 per 20-foot container.     In ports, bigger tends to be faster. Moving a container in a load of fewer than 500 takes about seven times as long as in one of more than 6,000. The gains go to big hubs, such as those in China, the Republic of Korea and Singapore, and the gap has widened since 2021.     Since 2006, the least developed countries’ access to regular shipping services has grown by 47%, against 17% elsewhere. But it started from a low base and is still about a third of the rest of the world’s. Their connectivity rankings have also fluctuated more over time.

    When the Strait of Hormuz was disrupted in February 2026, cargo shifted to other ports and moved on by road and rail, often across borders.

    One large carrier expanded overland services from ports in Saudi Arabia, the United Arab Emirates and Oman, and India cut paperwork for its diverted ships. How quickly goods move on such routes depends on customs and transit rules. Agreeing on them in advance and testing them could keep essential goods moving in a crisis.

    Yet in an UNCTAD survey of national trade facilitation committees, which bring government and business together, two thirds of respondents were unfamiliar with such planning.

    Ports also face a growing risk of cyberattacks: maritime cyber incidents reportedly doubled in 2025.

    In 2021, a container ship carrying hazardous chemicals caught fire and sank off Sri Lanka, polluting the sea and coastline. Ships carrying such cargo pass close to many coastal developing countries and small island developing states, where pollution can threaten livelihoods in fishing and tourism – even if those countries import little of it.

    From 29 November 2027, the 2010 Hazardous and Noxious Substances (HNS) Convention will establish an international system to compensate people and businesses for harm caused by hazardous cargo. Shipowners’ compulsory insurance pays first, and an international fund covers what it can’t.

    Companies in countries that have joined pay into the fund only if they receive hazardous cargo above set amounts. So countries that import little can join at little cost and still give their people access to compensation. Yet only 13 countries have joined, none of them small island developing states.

    The HNS Convention covers fuels such as ammonia and methanol when they are carried as cargo. Damage from using them to power or operate ships falls outside its scope. That gap will widen as more ships use alternative fuels. The International Maritime Organization (IMO) has set up a working group to address it.

    No binding international rules govern ship registration. By February 2026, 529 ships were falsely flying a country’s flag, about 45% of them a least developed country’s – a risk to safety, the marine environment and seafarers.

      - <strong>Policymakers should take part in the IMO’s work on who pays when alternative ship fuels cause harm.</strong> Use of these fuels is expected to grow as shipping works to cut its emissions.
