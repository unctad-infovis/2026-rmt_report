      ### The takeaway

      Key developments include several international conventions and agreements entering into force, covering marine biodiversity in areas beyond national jurisdiction, cross-border recognition of judicial sales of ships and, from November 2027, compensation for damage from hazardous cargo carried by sea.     The **2010 Hazardous and Noxious Substances (HNS)** Convention enters into force in November 2027. It will close a major legislative gap by establishing a comprehensive compensation regime for loss of life, personal injury, pollution damage and preventive measures arising from the carriage by sea of hazardous and noxious substances, such as chemicals, liquefied gases and some alternative fuels.

    The HNS Convention combines strict shipowner liability, backed by compulsory insurance, with a second tier of compensation financed by HNS cargo receivers through an international fund. The fund is set up under the convention and provides compensation of up to 250 million special drawing rights per incident.

    Acceding to the convention would be particularly beneficial for coastal and small island developing states that are highly vulnerable to HNS incidents but import little or no HNS cargo.

    In the absence of mandatory international rules to govern the ship registration process, the recently published, non-mandatory **International Maritime Organization (IMO) Guidelines on the Registration of Ships** provide important practical tools for verification, screening, information-sharing and registry oversight.

    Other important developments in 2026 include the entry into force of the landmark **BBNJ Agreement** to protect biodiversity in areas beyond national jurisdictions, of the **Beijing Convention on the International Effects of Judicial Sales of Ships**, and of the non-mandatory International Code of Safety for Maritime Autonomous Surface Ships **(MASS Code)**.

As the convention does not cover damage arising from the use of alternative fuels for ship propulsion and operation, policymakers should also engage actively in ongoing IMO work on this and related issues.
